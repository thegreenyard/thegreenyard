About This Florida Food Forest

A food forest does not need to occupy several acres to contain remarkable diversity. KC’s garden demonstrates how a relatively small residential property can become a densely layered edible landscape.

Rather than treating each fruit tree as an isolated specimen surrounded by open space, the garden uses close planting, vertical layers, and nearly every available growing area. The result is a living collection in which tropical fruit trees, shrubs, vines, and smaller edible plants grow together throughout the property.

During this tour, we explore the plants, layout, and growing decisions that allow KC to maintain such an extensive collection in a limited area. The garden offers ideas for anyone interested in increasing diversity without owning a large farm or homestead.

What Makes This Garden Abundant?

The defining feature of this food forest is its density. Instead of relying on a few large trees separated by open lawn, the garden fits many edible species into a compact residential landscape.

The abundance comes not only from the number of plants, but from the way the available space is used. Taller trees create an upper layer, smaller fruit trees and shrubs occupy the middle, while vines, herbs, and other edible plants fill additional niches.

KC’s garden shows that abundance can mean diversity, experimentation, and the ability to step outside and encounter something edible almost everywhere you look.

Key Ideas From the Tour

  1. A small residential property can support far more edible diversity than a conventional yard.
  2. Vertical layering helps growers make better use of limited land.
  3. Dense planting requires thoughtful placement and ongoing observation.
  4. A food forest can function as both a productive landscape and a living collection of unusual plants.
  5. Abundance does not always mean owning more land—it can mean using the space you already have more creatively.
