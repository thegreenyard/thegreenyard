Inside Cedar's Glendale Backyard Food Forest

Cedar's backyard is not designed around a single gardening formula. Instead, it has developed through years of observation, experimentation, pruning, transplanting, grafting, and learning how individual plants respond to Glendale's intense desert climate.

Traditional Arizona fruit trees grow beside tropical and subtropical species that many gardeners would not expect to find in the Sonoran Desert. Avocados, mangoes, bananas, papayas, lychees, longans, figs, grapes, pomegranates, citrus, mulberries, apples, and experimental seedlings all occupy carefully chosen spaces throughout the yard.

Some plants grow beneath established shade trees, while others receive full sun once they are mature enough to tolerate it. Mulberries, Arizona ash, citrus, desert trees, bananas, and other established plants help soften the summer heat for younger or more sensitive fruit trees. The resulting garden is a collection of small microclimates rather than one uniform growing space.

What makes this tour especially useful is Cedar's willingness to discuss the plants that struggled as openly as the plants that succeeded. He explains where trees were burned by the sun, damaged by frost, affected by wind, lost to root rot, attacked by pests, or simply failed to produce worthwhile fruit. Those experiences have shaped how the garden continues to evolve.

A Food Forest Built Through Experimentation

Cedar approaches the backyard as a living experiment. Some trees were planted because their fruit is already proven in Arizona, while others were added simply to discover whether they could survive the summer heat, winter cold, dry wind, and alkaline growing conditions.

Seedlings play an important role in that process. Mango, avocado, loquat, longan, peach, white sapote, and other seedlings are allowed to grow in pots or protected corners of the yard. If they remain healthy, they may eventually become permanent trees or provide vigorous rootstock for grafting improved varieties.

That approach reduces the pressure for every experiment to become an immediate success. A seedling that produces disappointing fruit may still become useful rootstock. A slow-growing tree may remain in a pot until a better location becomes available. A tropical species that survives without protection may reveal genetics worth preserving.

The garden therefore continues to change as Cedar learns which plants deserve more space, which need additional protection, and which may eventually be replaced by more productive trees.

Creating Shade and Microclimates in the Desert

Shade is one of the most important design tools in Cedar's backyard. Rather than covering the entire garden with permanent shade cloth, he uses a combination of established trees, temporary protection, plant placement, and layered growth to reduce heat stress where it matters most.

Mulberries are especially valuable because they grow rapidly and create summer shade for nearby mangoes, papayas, avocados, and other sensitive plants. Established desert trees, citrus, bananas, and an Arizona ash also contribute to the canopy, creating areas that receive morning sun, filtered afternoon light, or protection from the most intense western exposure.

Some trees receive temporary shade cloth during their first summers. Others are planted beneath an existing canopy with the expectation that they will eventually grow upward into stronger sunlight as they mature. This allows young trees to establish roots and develop woody growth before facing the full force of an Arizona summer.

Cedar also protects exposed branches and trunks with tree paint or mineral-based products such as Surround WP. These measures help reduce sunburn on tender new growth, especially when temperatures rise above 110 degrees or a tree suddenly pushes beyond the protection of the surrounding canopy.

Growing Avocados in Glendale

One of Cedar's favorite trees is a mature Fuerte avocado that spent its early years struggling with a weak, lanky central leader. The tree repeatedly leaned and failed to develop the stronger structure Cedar wanted.

After he removed the central leader, the tree responded by producing a vigorous side branch that became the new framework of the tree. Since then, it has developed thicker wood, larger leaves, stronger growth, and its first meaningful flowering period.

The avocado still requires protection from extreme heat and drying winds. Cedar uses light shade cloth during summer and protects newly exposed branches from sunburn. He has also attempted to graft Mexican avocado genetics onto the tree, hoping to introduce additional vigor, pollination potential, or climate tolerance.

Several avocado seedlings and young potted trees are also growing throughout the property. Some were planted intentionally, while others sprouted from discarded seeds. Even if these seedlings never produce exceptional fruit, they may become valuable rootstock for future grafting experiments.

Key lesson: A struggling avocado may respond better to structural pruning and improved placement than to additional fertilizer. In Cedar's garden, removing a weak central leader allowed the tree to redirect its energy into a much stronger framework.

Mangoes, Tropical Fruit, and Continuous Experimentation

Few plants better represent Cedar's gardening philosophy than the mangoes scattered throughout the backyard. Rather than relying on a single variety, he grows numerous cultivars in different locations, compares their performance, and continually experiments with grafting new genetics onto established trees.

Southern Blush, Nam Doc Mai, Coconut Cream, and several seedling mangoes all serve different purposes within the landscape. Some are grown for fruit quality, others for testing cold or heat tolerance, and some exist primarily as future rootstock capable of supporting multiple grafted varieties.

Several trees have already demonstrated how dramatically growth can change after structural pruning. Weak central leaders were removed, allowing stronger side branches to become the permanent framework of the tree. Others have been relocated into protected microclimates where surrounding trees reduce frost damage, wind stress, and summer sunburn.

Grafting is equally important. Cedar continually adds new scions from other growers, allowing one healthy tree to become home to several cultivars. Instead of planting dozens of full-sized mango trees, a single established root system can eventually produce many different varieties while saving valuable backyard space.

Layering Tropical Fruit Into the Landscape

Although the backyard sits in Arizona's Sonoran Desert, Cedar has carefully layered numerous tropical and subtropical species throughout the property. Bananas, papayas, Barbados cherries, loquats, longans, lychees, green sapote, figs, mulberries, citrus, and mangoes occupy different niches depending on their individual needs.

Instead of forcing every tropical tree into full desert sun, many begin life beneath existing shade trees or alongside larger plants that buffer heat and drying winds. As the trees mature, they gradually grow upward into brighter conditions while retaining cooler root zones below.

Even species considered difficult for Arizona—such as green sapote, lychee, papaya, and longan—are given opportunities to succeed through careful placement rather than assuming they cannot be grown at all. While not every experiment survives, each one provides information that helps shape future plantings throughout the garden.

Fruit Trees That Serve Multiple Purposes

Many of the larger trees perform far more than a single function. Mulberries provide summer shade while also producing fruit. Citrus trees create structure, biomass, and filtered light for neighboring tropical species. Grapes cool fences and trellises while producing heavy summer harvests. Groundcovers protect soil while contributing edible leaves and roots.

This stacking of functions is one of the defining characteristics of the food forest. Rather than assigning one purpose to each plant, Cedar looks for species that can contribute food, shade, habitat, biomass, rootstock, or future grafting opportunities at the same time.

As older trees mature, their role within the landscape may also change. Some eventually become permanent canopy trees, while others are pruned back or removed once neighboring tropical fruit can tolerate more exposure. The garden remains flexible instead of fixed.

Key lesson: Productive food forests are rarely built by getting everything right the first time. They evolve through years of experimentation, observation, and adapting to how each individual plant responds to its environment.

Lessons From Cedar's Food Forest

Perhaps the biggest takeaway from this backyard tour is that successful desert food forests are rarely built by following a rigid plan. Instead, they develop through years of observation, experimentation, and a willingness to continually adapt as plants mature.

Throughout the property, Cedar demonstrates how each tree influences the next. Shade trees protect tropical fruit, vigorous seedlings become future grafting stock, productive trees create biomass, and every season provides new information that shapes future decisions. Rather than seeing the landscape as finished, he views it as an evolving ecosystem.

For gardeners living in hot climates, this tour offers practical lessons that extend well beyond Arizona. Creating microclimates, protecting young trees, experimenting with different cultivars, and learning from both successes and failures are principles that can be applied almost anywhere.

Final takeaway: A productive food forest isn't created overnight. It grows one experiment at a time. Cedar's backyard shows that curiosity, observation, and patience are often just as important as choosing the right fruit tree.
