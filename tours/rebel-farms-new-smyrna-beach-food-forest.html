Rebel Farms is more than a collection of fruit trees. Tyson and Maggie are building an experiment in agriculture and community across a suburban neighborhood in New Smyrna Beach, Florida. Five neighboring families grow food together while testing which plants, cultivars, and growing systems can handle the area's heat, floods, freezes, and other challenges.

An Experimental Food Forest Built Around Community

Tyson describes Rebel Farms as an experiment in both agriculture and community. Five houses and five families live as neighbors, maintain their own kitchen gardens, trade what they grow, and work together on larger food production systems throughout the neighborhood.

The landscape is intentionally used as a testing ground. Citrus, bananas, avocados, figs, mangoes, starfruit, sapodillas, gingers, passion fruit, and other crops are planted in different conditions to see what actually works. The goal is not simply to grow an impressive collection of plants. Rebel Farms wants to identify crops that can produce reliably enough to eventually plant them on a much larger scale.

Learning From a Major Florida Freeze

One of the most valuable parts of this tour is seeing the food forest after a severe freeze rather than only seeing it at its best. Tyson explains that temperatures fell into roughly the 23–25°F range for several days. Bananas died back, mangoes were devastated, and several tropical crops were lost.

Instead of treating the losses only as a setback, Rebel Farms uses them as data. Avocados provide a great example. Lula had previously been a favorite, but Fantastic avocado trees came through the freeze far better and were already flowering and setting fruit afterward. That performance changed what Rebel Farms plans to plant as it expands.

Designing for Floods, Shade, and Florida Conditions

Cold is only one challenge here. Parts of Rebel Farms can receive several feet of floodwater, so the food forest also has to function under extremely wet conditions. Native oaks and palms remain overhead in parts of the property, where they act as windbreaks and reduce summer sun and water demand.

Below that canopy, the farm experiments with avocados, bananas, starfruit, Monstera, passion fruit, gingers, turmeric, citrus, and other edible plants. Paths help move water toward lower areas, while material cut from the food forest is returned to the soil through chop-and-drop.

From Chickens to Compost

Rebel Farms also tries to keep nutrients moving within the community. Families bring food scraps and organic material to the chickens, which help break it down and mix it with manure. That material is then moved into compost bins, finished, and eventually returned to whichever garden or growing area needs it.

Combined with chop-and-drop from the food forest itself, the system turns waste from the neighborhood into fertility for future food production.

The Green Yard Takeaway

What makes Rebel Farms especially interesting is the willingness to get things wrong. A freeze can wipe out years of tropical growth. A supposedly cold-hardy cultivar may fail. A different variety planted nearby may survive and immediately change the plan for the future.

That trial and error is the point. Rebel Farms is collecting real-world experience so future plantings can be based on what actually survives and produces in this climate. The ultimate goal is greater abundance—not just a food forest that looks productive, but growing systems capable of producing meaningful amounts of food for families and communities.
