Inside a Small Regenerative Florida Farm

Green Flamingo Organics has been growing on this Oak Hill property since 2009. The farm sells organic produce through a CSA, farmers market, health food stores, and local restaurants, but the most interesting part of the tour is how that food is produced.

Liz describes the farm as regenerative and no-till. Instead of relying on frequent soil disturbance, the farm uses cover crops, compost, tarps, hand tools, crop diversity, and living roots to build fertility and prepare beds. Even within individual production beds, multiple crops are often grown together.

The farm is also smaller than it may appear. Liz estimates the area they use at about 2.75 acres, and at any given time roughly half of that ground is in cover crop while the other half is used for production.

Growing More Through Diversity and Succession Planting

One of the clearest ideas in the tour is that production does not have to mean planting large blocks of a single crop. Liz shows beds containing carrots, turnips, kale, flowering kale, lettuce, and other crops together. That diversity is intentional: crops such as turnips can experience heavier insect pressure when planted in large uninterrupted rows, while mixing crops can reduce that pressure.

The same thinking applies over time. Green Flamingo succession-plants crops such as cilantro, radish, turnips, and carrots every three to four weeks instead of planting the entire season’s supply at once. For a home gardener, the same idea can extend harvests instead of having everything mature at once.

The farm also adjusts its crop mix based on years of experience. Liz explains that some mustards once used heavily in salad mix did not hold up as well through washing and bagging, so the farm shifted toward crops with stronger texture and better post-harvest quality.

Building Soil Without Tilling

Green Flamingo prepares production beds with tarps instead of tilling. Liz says that in their Florida climate, roughly three weeks under a tarp is often enough to eliminate most existing growth. When the tarp is removed, the dead plant material remains on the soil surface rather than being mechanically mixed into the ground.

Liz compares the process to lifting a log or rock in nature. The soil underneath stays cooler and moister, decomposition happens more slowly, and life continues beneath the covered surface. For transplants, a bed can often be planted directly after tarping; for tiny direct-seeded crops, surface material can be moved aside.

Leaving roots in the ground follows the same philosophy. Finished crops may remain temporarily as habitat for beneficial insects and as living or decomposing root material rather than being immediately pulled out.

Cover Crops, Seed Saving, and Farming for the Long Term

Green Flamingo devotes a large share of its limited acreage to soil-building. Liz explains that roughly half the farm is in cover crop at any given time. Some areas move through multiple rounds of summer and winter cover crops and rest for well over a year before returning to production.

Those cover-crop mixes are intentionally diverse, often containing nine to twelve species. Grasses such as wheat, barley, rye, and oats are combined with legumes such as peas, clovers, and alfalfa, while turnips and daikon radishes add roots and biomass. A flail mower eventually turns the standing cover crop into a mulch layer on the surface.

Seed saving adds another long-term layer. Liz has saved seed from one flowering kale variety for almost a decade and says it has become well adapted to the farm’s climate, soils, and the way the crop is harvested.

The Green Yard Takeaway

What stands out at Green Flamingo Organics is that regenerative farming is not presented as one special technique. It is a collection of choices: keeping living roots in the ground, rotating production with cover crops, diversifying beds, saving seed, preparing soil without tilling, and continually adjusting what is grown based on what the farm learns.

Those ideas are useful beyond a commercial farm. A backyard grower can succession-plant cilantro, mix crops instead of planting a single block, save seed from plants that perform well, leave roots in the soil, or use a cover crop during an off-season.
