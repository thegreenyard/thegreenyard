Kim’s Phoenix garden is proof that an abundant edible landscape does not require a huge property. Despite having only about 110 feet by 11 feet of plantable space, she has managed to fit 24 fruit trees into the property while also growing tropical ornamentals, vines, flowering plants, edible groundcovers, and support plants. The result is a constantly evolving small-space food forest shaped around Phoenix heat, afternoon shade, wind, limited room, and the plants Kim simply enjoys growing.

Inside a Tiny Phoenix Food Forest

Kim’s garden is built around one major limitation: space. The property is a corner lot, the backyard includes a large swimming pool, and much of the remaining plantable area consists of relatively narrow strips around the home. Instead of treating that as a reason to keep the landscape simple, Kim has used nearly every available pocket to grow something.

Fruit trees are tucked beside walls, near the pool, along the sides of the house, around the pond, and into the front yard. Containers add another layer where planting directly in the ground is not practical. Tropical ornamentals, flowering shrubs, vines, edible groundcovers, and temporary shade plants fill the spaces between the permanent trees.

The biggest surprise comes near the end of the tour, when Kim counts 24 fruit trees on the property. That number becomes even more impressive when she estimates that the usable planting area is only about 110 feet long by 11 feet wide. This is not abundance created by owning more land. It is abundance created by continually finding better ways to use the land that is available.

Designing Around Phoenix Heat and Afternoon Sun

One of the most useful parts of Kim’s tour is seeing how differently she treats each exposure around the house. Her corner lot receives intense sun and wind, and the western exposure can be especially difficult. The east side, however, becomes a valuable microclimate because the house begins shading it during the hottest part of summer afternoons.

Kim uses that protected side for many of her more tropical plants. The goal is not to eliminate Arizona heat, but to control when the plants receive it. Morning sun can provide plenty of light while afternoon shade reduces the intensity of the hottest June, July, and August conditions.

She also creates temporary shade rather than relying only on permanent structures. Fast-growing castor bean can provide seasonal protection. Vines cover the pergola during summer, shading mango trees below, and can then be removed when winter arrives so those same trees receive more sun.

Bamboo on the west side of the pergola adds another layer of protection, blocking late-afternoon sunlight that could still reach the mangoes after the overhead shade was in place. The garden shows how a small property can contain many different microclimates simply by paying attention to walls, structures, sun angles, and neighboring plants.

Keeping Fruit Trees Small on Purpose

A collection of 24 fruit trees would be difficult to maintain in this amount of space if every tree were allowed to reach its natural mature size. Kim instead treats pruning as part of the design. Several trees are deliberately kept compact so they remain compatible with the house, pool, walkways, and neighboring plants.

Her Pakistan mulberry is a good example of what happens when a vigorous tree outgrows the role available to it. Kim has repeatedly removed several feet of growth, yet the tree continues expanding rapidly. During the tour she explains that it may ultimately need to leave because it simply wants more space than the property can comfortably provide.

Other trees fit the system better when kept small. The limequat can be pruned within reach. White sapote, guava, citrus, Barbados cherry, mango, and other fruit trees are managed at a scale that makes sense for a residential yard. The goal is not to see how large each tree can become. It is to keep many different trees productive within the same limited landscape.

Mangoes Growing in Kim’s Phoenix Garden

Mangoes are scattered throughout the property and tell several different stories. Some began as nursery discards that looked nearly dead but still had enough life to recover. One mango in the front yard has stayed relatively small while producing heavily, demonstrating why Kim does not necessarily associate a large tree with a better harvest.

On the east side, a Carrie mango is recovering after a period when the garden received less attention. Another mango, planted around 2017, once grew above the roofline before declining badly. Kim later uncovered a root flare that had become buried, increased care, added protection, and watched the tree begin pushing strong new growth.

Near the pond, Kim originally planned a larger mango planting. As her interest in the pond grew, the water feature expanded and the mango area became smaller. Three mangoes remain in that zone, where summer vines create overhead shade and winter removal allows more sunlight back into the space.

The pond also contributes irrigation. Kim periodically lowers the water level, stirs nutrient-rich material from the fish system, and pumps the pond water toward nearby mangoes. It is another example of how different parts of the garden overlap instead of operating as isolated features.

Unusual Fruit in a Small Arizona Backyard

The variety of fruit trees is what makes Kim’s small property especially interesting. Alongside mangoes and familiar citrus are fruits that many people would not expect to see growing in a Phoenix backyard.

A limequat is one of Kim’s favorites. She describes eating the small fruit whole, with a sweet, aromatic peel surrounding a tart interior. The tree can become larger, but she keeps it compact because space is limited.

Her starfruit has been particularly productive, fruiting repeatedly and earning a permanent place in the garden. A young longan is also growing in a surprisingly sunny location. Kim originally expected it to need more protection, but the tree has handled the site better than anticipated.

A Redlands white sapote, white guava, Ruby Supreme guava, Barbados cherry, papaya, kumquats, mandarins, oranges, lemon, peach, mulberry, and other fruit trees add even more diversity. The collection is less about creating rows of one crop and more about seeing how many different edible plants can occupy useful niches around the property.

Using Plants for More Than Fruit

Kim often thinks about plants in terms of the jobs they can perform. A fruit tree may also become shade for the house. Bamboo can protect tropical plants while screening an unwanted view. Sweet potato covers exposed soil. Castor bean can quickly create temporary summer shade. Flowering plants can attract pollinators while making the landscape more enjoyable.

The Barbados cherry near the house is allowed to fill a specific space because it helps block early sunlight from reaching a bedroom. Elsewhere, a guava is being positioned with future shade in mind. These decisions matter more in a small garden because there is little room for plants that do only one thing.

That multifunctional approach also gives the yard the layered feeling of a food forest. Fruit trees form the permanent framework, but the spaces underneath and between them are occupied by sweet potato, hibiscus, tropical milkweed, plumerias, vines, monstera, elephant food, and other plants with different roles.

The Pond That Changed the Garden Plan

Kim’s pond is a good reminder that gardens do not have to follow the original plan forever. The water feature began much smaller, while the surrounding area was intended to hold more mango trees. Over time Kim discovered that she loved the pond, so it expanded and several mangoes were removed.

That decision reduced the number of fruit trees that could occupy the space, but it made the backyard more enjoyable for the person who actually lives there. The pond now provides movement, water, fish, a focal point, and a source of nutrient-rich irrigation for nearby plants.

In a garden where every square foot matters, choosing to devote more room to a pond might seem inefficient if fruit production were the only goal. Kim’s yard shows a broader version of abundance: a productive landscape can also be a place that is beautiful, relaxing, interesting, and personally meaningful.

A Food Forest in Recovery

Kim is also open about the fact that the garden has gone through a difficult period. During a challenging time in her life, many plants received far less care than they normally would have. Some trees were lost, others declined, and several plants shown during the tour are still rebuilding.

She describes the property as a “recovery yard.” That makes the tour a useful counterpoint to the idea that a food forest should always look finished. Gardens change as the lives of their growers change. Trees can struggle, irrigation can be missed, priorities can shift, and a landscape can still recover.

The older mango that declined and began pushing new growth is one example. Kim’s Carrie mango is another. Other areas are being reconsidered entirely, with plants moved, replaced, or removed when they no longer fit the available space.

Rather than hiding those changes, the tour documents them. The garden becomes a record of experimentation, setbacks, recovery, and the willingness to keep adjusting.

The Green Yard Takeaway

Kim’s garden demonstrates that abundance is not simply a matter of owning more land. With roughly 110 feet by 11 feet of plantable space, she has managed to fit 24 fruit trees into the property while still making room for tropical plants, flowers, vines, groundcovers, a swimming pool, and a pond.

The bigger lesson is how she uses that limited space. Trees are kept small, afternoon shade is treated as a resource, temporary plants create seasonal microclimates, and individual plants often perform several jobs at once. Even the parts of the garden that are recovering or changing become part of the story. Kim’s yard shows that a small edible landscape can still hold enormous diversity when the gardener is willing to experiment and continually adapt.
