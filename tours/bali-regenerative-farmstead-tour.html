In the mountains of central Bali, just a kilometer from a protected ancient rainforest, Hayley and local farmer Made are transforming former monocropped land into a diverse regenerative garden. Vegetables, herbs, flowers and tropical crops share space with bees, butterflies and beneficial insects. Joined by my son Sebastian, I explore a garden where experimentation, observation and a connection with nature are as important as the food being harvested.

Inside Bali Regenerative Farmstead

Hayley describes the farmstead as a passion project that began five or six years before our visit. Having lived in Bali for 13 years, she wanted to build a life that allowed her to thrive with nature. The property sits about 700 meters above sea level, where she estimates temperatures are around six degrees Celsius cooler than lower areas of the island.

The nearby rainforest provides inspiration for the landscape she hopes to create. Rather than operating solely as a conventional production farm, Hayley sees the property as a place to demonstrate how a diverse ecosystem can produce food throughout the year.

Our first tour focuses on the vegetable and herb garden, where familiar crops grow alongside tropical alternatives. The garden is still evolving, and the successes and challenges Hayley shares make it particularly interesting to explore.

Transforming Monocropped Land Into a Thriving Ecosystem

One of the most memorable stories from the tour begins with hundreds of caterpillars. When Hayley and Made first planted salad greens on land previously managed with chemicals, the young plants attracted an overwhelming number of caterpillars. Made was understandably concerned, but Hayley wanted to observe what would happen before intervening.

Several weeks later, Made sent Hayley a video of a wasp feeding on a caterpillar. Ants also began taking caterpillars, and Hayley says the garden has not experienced the same problem since. This is an account of what happened on their property, rather than a guarantee that every pest problem will resolve without intervention.

Flowers such as zinnias and marigolds are woven through the vegetable beds. Some crops are allowed to flower and set seed, providing resources for insects as well as future plantings. Hayley watches for ladybirds and praying mantises as signs of predator activity.

Made is central to the story. Hayley credits his openness to new methods, enthusiasm for regenerative agriculture and ongoing work with much of the garden’s progress. Their partnership illustrates how experimentation and local knowledge can develop together.

Adapting to Bali’s Tropical Growing Conditions

Growing familiar vegetables in a tropical climate has required Hayley to rethink some of her favorite crops. Strawberries struggle with heavy rain, while tomatoes have been more successful during the extended dry season. During our visit, we taste both red and golden cherry tomatoes, and Hayley discusses her hope of eventually growing tomatoes under cover during the wet season.

Eggplants offer one of the most striking examples of adaptation. Earlier attempts produced only a few fruits before they rotted or fell. Grafting cultivated purple eggplants onto a locally adapted, spiny eggplant rootstock has given Hayley a much more continuous supply. Made is preparing another graft during the tour.

Red and green amaranth, known locally as bayam, have become alternatives to the spinach Hayley grew up eating. Sweet potatoes have similarly become a more successful crop for her than ordinary potatoes. These choices show how a garden can evolve around the plants that perform well in its own climate.

The herb garden adds another dimension. Hayley combines chocolate mint with stevia for a sweet tasting experience and tarragon with stevia for a licorice- like flavor. Lemon balm, dill, garlic chives and mitsuba provide additional variety. She also saves seeds from her salad crops and says she has not purchased seeds in a couple of years.

Growing Toward Self-Sufficiency and Regenerative Education

Hayley hopes to reach around 80 percent self-sufficiency at the farmstead. Her first soybean experiment is particularly personal: she would love to produce the soybeans needed for her own tempeh. Pineapples, cassava and several kinds of sweet potato already add diversity to the garden.

Vetiver grass illustrates her interest in plants that perform multiple functions. At the farmstead it helps stabilize the garden edges, supplies cut material for mulch and is planted near the greywater system. Torch ginger also connects the garden to local culinary traditions through its use in sambal.

Looking ahead, Hayley envisions a syntropic agroforestry demonstration inspired by the nearby rainforest, an educational center, a farm café and potentially a soil laboratory. She imagines local farmers bringing soil samples to observe microbial life under a microscope. These are future ambitions rather than completed facilities.

Her childhood experiences in the rainforest on Australia’s Christmas Island and her interest in the Ringing Cedars book series have helped shape her personal connection with nature. The tour offers a glimpse of how those influences inform the farmstead’s evolving philosophy.

The Green Yard Takeaway

What stands out about Hayley and Made’s garden is not just the diversity of food growing there, but the willingness to observe and adapt. From grafting eggplants to saving seeds and watching insects establish relationships, the farmstead is a living experiment shaped by its surroundings.

Abundance can mean more than a harvest. It can also mean a landscape filled with life, a partnership that builds knowledge, and a place where others can learn. Bali Regenerative Farmstead offers an inspiring example of that vision as it continues to develop.
