Growing in Arizona

One State, Very Different Growing Climates

Arizona ranges from extremely hot low-desert communities to much cooler high-elevation regions. That means there is no single Arizona growing climate. The food forests documented by The Green Yard are currently concentrated in the Phoenix metropolitan area, where long, intensely hot summers are paired with mild winters and occasional freezes.

In the low desert, successful edible landscapes often depend on thoughtful placement, shade, mulch, irrigation, soil improvement, and microclimates. Winter sun can be valuable, while harsh summer afternoon sun may need to be reduced for subtropical and tropical plants.

These constraints also create unusual opportunities. Citrus, dates, pomegranates, figs, mulberries, stone fruit, and many other crops can thrive, while adventurous growers continue testing mango, avocado, guava, white sapote, and other subtropical fruit in protected locations.

Climate Snapshot

Arizona Low-Desert Growing Conditions

These conditions describe the Phoenix-area low desert where most of The Green Yard’s current Arizona tours are located.

Climate Hot Desert

Extremely hot, dry summers with abundant sunshine and mild winter conditions.

Growing Season Nearly Year-Round

Different crops peak in different seasons, with winter often providing excellent vegetable-growing weather.

Summer Extreme Heat

Temperatures above 110°F are possible and afternoon sun can be especially stressful for sensitive plants.

Winter Mild With Freeze Risk

Most winter days are mild, but occasional cold nights can damage tropical and subtropical plants.

Water Low Rainfall

Reliable irrigation, deep watering, mulch, and water-efficient design are central to productive landscapes.

Best Strategy Build Microclimates

Shade, canopy, wind protection, thermal mass, and careful plant placement can significantly expand what is possible.

The Growing Reality

Why Grow Food in the Arizona Desert?

Growing Opportunities

  • Abundant winter sunshine and a long overall growing season
  • Excellent conditions for citrus, dates, figs, and pomegranates
  • Low humidity can reduce some fungal disease pressure
  • Warm winters allow many subtropical plants to remain evergreen
  • Microclimates can support surprising tropical fruit experiments

Growing Challenges

  • Extreme summer heat and intense afternoon sun
  • Very low natural rainfall and high irrigation demand
  • Alkaline soils and water can create nutrient-management issues
  • Occasional winter freezes can damage sensitive tropical plants
  • Young trees often need protection while establishing
